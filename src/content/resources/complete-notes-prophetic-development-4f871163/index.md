Preoccupation With God Rather Than Gift<br>
Tom Bedford<br>
The idea here is very simple but has proven to live out with great difficulty. This is due to the reality that prophetic gifting and ability create a measure of light and stature on the individuals who oper- ate in this grace. As people everywhere are trying to discover the divine and break into the realm of the spirit, anyone who carries a measure of prophetic grace will receive focus and attention. This will cause people to look to ministers who can manifest the divine. It is therefore extremely impor- tant that those ministers remember the attention given them is for the purposes of God and not for the validation of their humanity or the securing of identity. Identity must come from the Lord in order to remain balanced so that when the temptation comes to elevate prophetic ministers, that elevation does not distract them from The Lord. This is a cause of great stumbling in the light of the Glory of God as they face the temptation to take it for their own.<br>
Ultimately, as the Psalmist said in Chapter 16, The Lord is our first preoccupation and our portion.<br>
In other words, our satisfaction in life comes from Him first as we love Him first. Anything that comes before Him that we want to protect or promote beyond what He is doing in our lives runs the risk of becoming an idol that is destined to fall.

2\. Strategic prophets and apostles<br>

## Three Hebrew words:

Prophecy comprehends three things: prediction; singing by the dictate of the Spirit; and understanding and explaining the mysterious, hidden sense of Scripture by an immediate illumination and motion of the Spirit.

The Old Testament uses three Hebrew words that are translated into the English word “prophet” or “seer”: nabi, roeh, and hozeh.

The English word prophet comes from the Greek word προφήτης (profétés) meaning advocate.

Nabi is the first and the most generally used for a prophet.<br>
The Hebrew word derives from a verb signifying “to bubble forth” like a fountain; hence the word means one who announces or pours forth the declarations of God. The English word comes from the Greek prophetes (profetes ), which signifies in classical Greek one who speaks for another, especially one who speaks for a god, and so interprets his will to man; hence its essential meaning is<br>
“an interpreter.”

In the time of Samuel another word, ro’eh, “seer,” began to be used (<span class="scripture-reference">1 Sam. 9:9</span>). It occurs seven times in reference to Samuel.

Afterwards another word, hozeh, “seer” (<span class="scripture-reference">2 Sam. 24:11</span>), was employed. In <span class="scripture-reference">1 Chronicles 29:29</span> all these three words are used: “Samuel the seer (ro’eh), Nathan the prophet (nabi’), Gad the seer”<br>
(hozeh). In <span class="scripture-reference">Josh. 13:22</span> Balaam is called (Hebrew) a kosem “diviner,” a word used only of a false prophet.

1\. ro’eh = “seer” (BDB 906), <span class="scripture-reference">1 Sam. 9:9</span>. Ro’eh is from the general Hebrew term “to see.” This person understood God’s ways and plans and was consulted to ascertain God’s will in a matter.<br>
Roeh means “to see” or “to perceive.” It is generally used to describe one who is a revealer of secrets, one who envisions.


Valuing Spirit and Truth on Mission<br>
2\. hozeh = “seer” <span class="scripture-reference">2 Sam. 24:11</span>; <span class="scripture-reference">Amos 7:12</span>. It is from a rarer Hebrew term “to see in a vision.”<br>
It also means “to see” or “to perceive,” but is also used in reference to musicians. It is used to describe a counselor or an advisor to a king. The Hebrew does not necessarily indicate that the person is a prophet, but rather an advisor—someone who has wisdom. It means “one who has insight.” The translators try to indicate whether the message is spiritual. If it is spiritual, then they tend to translate hozeh as “prophet.”

If it does not give any indication of being spiritually generated, then they would render it “advisor” or “counselor.”<br>
3\. nabi’ = “prophet” (BDB 611), cognate of Akkadian verb nabu = “to call” and Arabic naba’a<br>
= “to announce.” This is the most common OT term to designate a prophet. It is used over 300 times. A prophet is someone who speaks for God to His people (cf. <span class="scripture-reference">Amos 3:8</span>; <span class="scripture-reference">Jeremiah 1:7,17</span>;
<span class="scripture-reference">Ezekiel 3:4</span>).

Nabi literally means “to bubble up.” It describes one who is stirred up in spirit. It is the most frequently used of the three by the Hebrew writers. When the sense of “bubbling up” is applied to speaking, it becomes “to declare.” Hence, a nabi, or a prophet, is an announcer—one who pours forth the declarations of God.

4\. All three terms are used of the prophet’s office in <span class="scripture-reference">1 Chronicles 29:29</span>; Samuel - Ro’eh; Nathan -<br>
Nabi’; and Gad - Hozeh.
